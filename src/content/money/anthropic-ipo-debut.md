Three weeks ago I broke down [Anthropic's S-1](/money/anthropic-ant-s1-ipo/) and asked whether a $2 trillion ask made sense. Today the market answered — louder and stranger than anyone expected. $ANT priced at $83 on Wednesday night, opened Thursday at $111.50, briefly brushed a $2 trillion valuation, and then spent the next two days round-tripping: by Friday's close it had traded all the way back to its IPO price before settling at $88.91 — up 7% from the IPO price, down 20% from the open.

Here's the day, the payday, the tab, and the math.

## The largest IPO in history, by every measure

The mechanics, quickly:

- **Price:** $83 per share, valuing Anthropic at **$1.5 trillion** at pricing — the third-largest company to go public in US history on day one, behind only the biggest mega-caps.
- **Size:** the IPO raised **$81.3 billion** — the largest offering ever, smashing SpaceX's record. Including a concurrent private placement, the total raise was **$107.3 billion**.
- **Demand:** the book was reportedly **~6x oversubscribed** (Bloomberg), with Japanese investors alone seeking $9.4B of stock. The pound fell to a 2026 low as overseas buyers converted into dollars to get their allocations.
- **Day one:** opened $111.50 (+34%), traded as high as $115.10, and spent the afternoon fading. The first trade alone was worth ~$12B of stock. If you got an allocation, you're up 15%. If you bought the open, you're down 15%. Welcome to price discovery.
- **The week:** the pop didn't hold. Friday the stock round-tripped almost entirely — touching $83.05, right back at the IPO price — before recovering to close the week at $88.91. Cathie Wood bought roughly $146M of the dip through her funds, and by Monday the options market and a small army of 2x leveraged ETFs (Tradr, Direxion, T-REX, ProShares, Leverage Shares) had opened for business. A company three days old already has more derivatives on it than most fifty-year-olds.
- **The skeptics showed up too:** Aswath Damodaran — the NYU professor who literally wrote the valuation textbooks — published an intrinsic value of **$76 a share, below the IPO price**, the same morning the stock traded 34% above it.

For context on how unprecedented this is: the previous record was SpaceX. Anthropic raised more in one morning than most countries' annual infrastructure budgets. And it did it as a Public Benefit Corporation where a trust, not shareholders, controls the board.

## The payday: the greatest wealth-creation event Silicon Valley has seen

Now the part everyone's talking about. At the indicated price, the average Anthropic employee is worth roughly **$44 million**. The full breakdown:

- **7 co-founders: ~$20+ billion each**
- **Several early employees: $1+ billion each**
- **598 employees: $100+ million each**
- **2,800 employees: $1+ million each**
- **Total employee equity wealth: $232 billion**, including **$119 billion** of stock-option gains

Read that fourth line again. Nearly six hundred *non-founder* employees just crossed nine figures. Thousands of new millionaires were minted before lunch on a Thursday.

The ripple effects are already visible in the strangest place: housing. San Francisco's median single-family home sale price jumped **+29% year over year to $2.39 million in October** — amid the IPO process — the largest monthly-market jump since February 2018. The IPO hasn't even closed escrow on its first trade and it's already repriced one of America's most expensive cities.

And in keeping with the PBC structure: TechCrunch estimates the IPO could deliver **$156 billion to charity** over time through Anthropic's giving commitments and employee pledges. Even the windfall has a mission statement.

## The $900B tab: the number the S-1 was hiding in plain sight

Here's what changes my S-1 analysis most. When I wrote the breakdown, the *public* filing showed **$154.9B** of verified non-cancelable compute commitments (Amazon $110B, Microsoft $44.9B, Google redacted), with press estimates north of $200B. I called it a $200B compute tab and a mortgage you can't refinance.

I lowballed it. Anthropic's updated IPO filings revealed the real figure: **nearly $900 billion in long-term spending commitments** — compute, power, data centers — with reports suggesting obligations stretch **as far as 2045**. That's not a mortgage; that's a small country's GDP in purchase orders.

Put the tab against the business:

| | |
|---|---|
| Total commitments | ~$900B |
| Anthropic TTM revenue | ~$35B |
| Commitments as multiple of annual revenue | **~25x** |
| Average annual bill if spread over 20 years | ~$45B/yr |
| Average annual bill if spread over 10 years | ~$90B/yr |

Notice what the table is really saying: **even spread over two decades, the average annual bill exceeds today's entire revenue.** Anthropic has pre-ordered compute that costs more per year than the company currently earns in total — before payroll, before research, before the lights.

This is why the growth rate matters more here than at any company I've ever written about. Anthropic doesn't need to grow 1,300% forever — nobody expected that to persist, and it's already compressing (H1 2026 grew 1,300%; Q3 grew "only" ~12x, roughly 1,100%, per the updated disclosures). Percentage growth *always* decays as the base swells. The question isn't the decay. The question is the **steady state**: what level of growth does the business settle into once the law of large numbers takes over? If Anthropic compounds into a $300B-, $500B-, $700B-revenue company, the $900B tab is a rounding error on the way up. If it plateaus at $80B of revenue — a spectacular, top-ten-software-company outcome by any historical standard — the tab is a slow-motion crisis, because $45-90B a year of fixed bills against $80B of revenue leaves nothing for anything else.

That's the whole company in one sentence: **Anthropic needs to become one of the largest companies in economic history just to make its existing purchase orders look normal.**

And Wall Street isn't waiting around for the answer: per the WSJ, credit analysts already expect Anthropic's debt to be rated **junk** — with the rating set to shape its capital plans and, arguably, the future of the AI industry. The equity market spent IPO week celebrating the growth story; the credit market spent it asking who pays when the fixed tab meets a revenue curve that bends.

## What we now know financially

The IPO process pried loose numbers the S-1 kept redacted:

- **TTM revenue: ~$35.3B.** For scale: that's roughly a third of NVIDIA's *quarterly* data-center revenue — but growing an order of magnitude faster.
- **Q3 2026 revenue up ~12x year over year.** Consistent with the ~$16.8B quarter that was redacted in the public filing.
- **Operating losses still shrinking** toward breakeven even while spending aggressively, and operating cash flow was positive (+$3.9B in H1) *before* the raise.
- **The war chest is now absurd:** ~$131.7B pre-IPO plus $81.3B raised (and change from the private placement) — call it **~$210B+ of cash**. That's more liquid firepower than most sovereign wealth funds. The $900B tab gets paid out of this, one year at a time.
- **13.22B shares outstanding**; the TTM "net loss" of ~$121B remains mostly the fair-value accounting fiction I walked through last time.

## The reverse DCF, take two: now we have a real price

Last time I ran a reverse DCF against a hypothetical $2T valuation. Now the market has voted. Setup: at ~$95, Anthropic's market cap is ~$1.26T; less ~$210B of post-IPO cash, call it roughly **$1.05T of enterprise value**. (Caveat: the $108.6B of convertible notes muddies true EV in both directions — they're dilution more than debt — so take the precision with salt.) Base year: $35.3B TTM revenue, FCF margin ramping from today's ~2%. Ten-year model, 12% discount rate, 4% terminal growth:

| Scenario | 10-yr revenue path | FY36 FCF margin | Implied EV | vs. ~$1.05T today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stall** | 80% decaying to 12% | 12% | ~$1.16T | ~+11% |
| **Harsh bear** | 60% decaying to 10% | 15% | ~$0.68T | ~−35% |
| **Bear** | 100% decaying to 15% | 20% | ~$3.98T | ~+279% |
| **Bull** | 100% for 2 yrs, then 25% | 30% | ~$7.99T | ~+661% |
| **Market-implied** | **~34% every year** | 30% | ~$1.05T | — |

Look at the first row. **My stall scenario — growth collapsing from 80% to 12%, margins barely reaching software-company levels — now values Anthropic *above* its current market price.** When I ran this math against the $2T target three weeks ago, stall meant −88%. The first-week fade has repriced $ANT to almost exactly "the boom stalls but doesn't die." (By Friday's close of $88.91 — market cap ~$1.19T, EV ~$0.98T — the picture sharpens further: implied growth ~33%/yr, and the stall scenario sits ~19% above the market.)

The market-implied path: **~34% revenue growth compounded for a decade**, reaching ~$650B of revenue by FY36 — 19x today, which would make Anthropic one of the five largest companies on Earth by revenue. At a friendlier 9% discount rate the required growth drops to ~25% — still a decade of mega-cap growth rates from a company one-thirtieth the size of the companies it's being compared to.

Is that crazy? History says the base rate is brutal — almost nothing compounds at 34% for ten years, and the companies that did (Amazon, Apple, NVIDIA) all went through stretches that would have shaken out here. But the base rate for *everything about this company* is brutal: the growth, the raise, the tab. The bet isn't really "can Anthropic grow 34%." It's "does pay-for-intelligence become the next layer of the economy." If yes, $900B of commitments was the bargain of the century. If no, it was the most expensive wish list ever filed with the SEC.

## Bottom line

Day one of $ANT delivered everything IPO days are supposed to deliver: a record raise, a 34% pop, nine-figure paper wealth for six hundred employees, a city repriced. What it also delivered — quietly, in the updated filings — is the full size of the bill: ~$900B of commitments that only pay off if Anthropic becomes one of the biggest revenue machines in history. The stock spent today arguing about exactly that: opening like the bull case was certain, closing like the stall case was live. Both were right for a few hours. The next ten years will pick the winner.
