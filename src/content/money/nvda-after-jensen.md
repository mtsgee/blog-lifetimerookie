Jensen Huang resigned as NVIDIA's CEO on November 11, 2026, and the stock went into tonight's Q3 report near its 52-week low of $148.80. The report just landed — revenue up 103%, guidance well above consensus — and the stock is up about 3% after hours. My take up front: NVIDIA absolutely survives this. CUDA is still the moat, the chips still lead. But the "Jensen premium" — the extra multiple the market paid for the man's market-making and aura — is gone, and it's not coming back under an interim CEO.

One ground rule before we start: I'm not litigating whether he should have stayed or whether the board was right. This post is about the facts and how to think about $NVDA from here.

## Update, Monday November 23: He's back. The AI trade is BACK.

Forget everything I just wrote — because the board already did. This morning, [NVIDIA announced](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director) that Jensen Huang is reinstated as President, CEO, and board director, effective immediately. Six days after publicly ruling his return out. Eleven days after accepting his resignation. The vote was unanimous.

The details matter, because they're almost as wild as the reversal itself:

- **The board's reasoning:** after a series of discussions, it was "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead" — and, crucially, it *received input from senior levels of the United States government* before concluding his reinstatement "poses no national security risk." The CEO of America's most important chip company came back, in part, on Washington's say-so.
- **Trump says he made it happen.** In a CNBC interview after the announcement, the president said he personally "convinced" the board — while lawmakers from both parties trashed the decision, with one calling it a "failure of character." A bipartisan Congress is furious that the man who resigned amid a foreign-intelligence scandal is back, and Jensen is scheduled to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3 — on export controls, of all things.
- **The guardrails are real.** The independent-director investigation continues to conclusion, a new National Security and Export Control Committee has been formed, and NVIDIA is hiring its first Chief Compliance Officer. Harvey Jones stays as chair; Kress returns to the CFO job she never really stopped doing.
- **The market's verdict was immediate.** The stock hadn't stabilized after earnings — it slid all last week to fresh lows ($124.50), well below the $148.80 level I wrote about below. This morning it ripped **+30%** intraday to ~$164, an open at $167. The whole AI complex went with it: CoreWeave, Nebius, and IREN all up 20%+, the Nasdaq surging after the S&P's seven-day losing streak.

Here's the number I can't stop thinking about: between Thursday's close and this morning's high, roughly **half a trillion dollars of market value** swung on the news that one man got his desk chair back. The "Jensen premium" I wrote about below isn't gone — it never was. It was just priced wrong, twice, in two different directions, eleven days apart.

My first tell from the bottom line below — "who gets the permanent CEO job" — got answered in the loudest way possible. Tells two and three are still live: whether the checkbook stays closed (tell #2), and whether the loyalists who threatened to walk actually stay (tell #3). And a new one: whether the compliance machinery being built around Jensen changes how he does the market-making that made him irreplaceable in the first place.

Everything below is the post as I wrote it through the November 17 earnings print. I'm leaving it standing, because the whiplash *is* the story — but read it now knowing how it ends.

## What happened

The timeline, quickly:

- **November 11, 2026:** NVIDIA's board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation as President, CEO, and board member, effective immediately (SEC 8-K).
- **The context:** per AP, the New York Times, and others, the resignation followed revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman linked to Chinese intelligence — a national-security-grade problem for the company at the center of US export controls. A board investigation is ongoing.
- **Interim CEO:** Colette Kress, NVIDIA's CFO since September 2013 (ex-Cisco, ex-Microsoft), takes over while keeping the CFO job. Harvey C. Jones — on the board since 1993 and former Synopsys CEO — becomes NVIDIA's first-ever Chair of the Board.
- **The succession race:** the board hired Heidrick & Struggles and is looking at internal and external candidates; names floated include NVIDIA's Ian Buck and Arm CEO Rene Haas (WSJ).
- **The twist, today:** this morning Jensen released his first statement since resigning — he'd be "honored" to return as CEO if the board decides it's in the company's best interest, adding "I own my mistakes completely" and citing an outpouring of support from employees. The board is reportedly not discussing it (WSJ/Bloomberg/CNBC) — and on tonight's earnings call, the chairman said outright that the board has no intention of reinstating him.
- **And tonight:** NVIDIA reported Q3 FY27 — the first print under an interim CEO. The numbers are below.

## What the numbers show — and what they don't

The business did not get the memo. Q3 FY27, NVIDIA's first print under an interim CEO:

- **Revenue: $115.8B, up 103% year over year**, versus roughly $109B of consensus. Adjusted EPS of $2.68 beat the $2.47 street by 8%.
- **Data Center: $107.6B, up 110%** year over year and 21% quarter over quarter.
- **Guidance: $130B ± 3% for Q4** — roughly another doubling at the midpoint, comfortably above the ~$125B consensus — and it assumes **zero data-center compute revenue from China**. The full-year FY28 outlook was raised from +70% growth to +75%.
- **Vera Rubin is shipping.** The architecture the whole buildout has been waiting on is ramping, per management.
- **The backlog got bigger, not smaller:** the cloud-industry backlog now sits at **$2.5T, up from $2T three months ago**. NVIDIA's own supply commitments rose to **$390B, from $279B** last quarter, and its neocloud agreements now total **$32B**.

One nuance worth flagging, because it cuts against a story I told above: I said the checkbook closes without Jensen — but supply commitments (NVIDIA pre-buying wafers, memory, and capacity *for itself*) went up 40% in a quarter. That's not customer financing; it's a confidence signal. Kress can refuse to underwrite other people's GPUs while still prepaying for her own.

And what the numbers don't tell us: anything about succession. A blowout quarter is backward-looking validation of the machine Jensen built. It says nothing about who runs it next, whether his loyalists stay, or how long an interim CEO-who-is-also-CFO can keep making the handshake deals that never show up on an income statement. Mid-print, the WSJ reported the SEC has opened a civil probe into whether NVIDIA's disclosures misled investors — the Singapore compliance question just got a regulator attached to it.

## What Jensen actually was

It's hard to overstate what NVIDIA lost. Jensen didn't just run the company; he *made markets*. The handshake deals with suppliers, cloud providers, and sovereign funds formed a moat that never showed up in a patent filing. CUDA locked in the developers; Jensen locked in everything else.

The checkbook was part of the flywheel: NVIDIA joined a record **129 venture deals over the past year, versus 49 in 2023**, and its last quarterly filing listed **$29B of cloud service agreements and $279B of supply and capacity commitments** (WSJ, via Semi-Doped):

![Nvidia venture deals per year: 49 in 2023 vs 129 in the past year. Data: WSJ via Semi-Doped](/images/nvda-checkbook.png)

My standing rule is founders over hired CEOs — founders carry the company's DNA and can pull levers a finance-first executive can't. Kress is a genuinely excellent operator who's been on every earnings call for 13 years. But "key architect of the capital strategy" (the board chair's words) is a different job than inventing markets. Nobody does what Jensen did with a spreadsheet.

## What changes without him

The strategy shift is already visible:

- **The checkbook closes.** Kress is expected to rein in NVIDIA's financing of the AI industry (WSJ), and Jensen had already started pulling back — at GTC Berlin in October he told neocloud CEOs to secure upfront payments from their own customers, reportedly quipping "I could just give all this capacity to Elon!" UBS's Tim Arcuri estimates a financing pullback could take **$50B or more** out of the market, and lenders are already tighter — Blackstone's QTS cut a planned private debt sale by ~60% to roughly $300M after investors balked at pricing (Semi-Doped).
- **The braindrain risk is real.** A meaningful slice of NVIDIA's talent is loyal to Jensen personally — the calls for his return from employees are already public. Meta, Amazon, and Google will be recruiting hard while the ship lacks a captain.
- **Competition smells blood.** AMD, the custom-silicon programs at the hyperscalers, and every well-funded chip startup now get to sell against a company in transition. The moat doesn't evaporate today — but moats erode at the edges, and erosion compounds.
- **Export-control exposure stays hot.** The FT reported NVIDIA's Singapore subsidiary booked $104B in 2025 revenue (+72% YoY), and compliance questions are swirling. That file now lands on a CEO who is also still the CFO.

## What doesn't change

- **CUDA is still the moat.** The software lock-in that took a decade to build doesn't unwind because the CEO resigned.
- **The chips still lead.** The data-center roadmap ships regardless of who's at the podium.
- **The financial engine is absurd.** 74.7% gross margins, $127B of free cash flow (a 41.9% margin) over the trailing twelve months, $23.6B of net cash (S&P Global). Balance sheets don't have scandals.
- **Demand is still demand.** The quarter that just landed — +103% revenue, an 8% EPS beat, guidance to another near-doubling — would be a career-best print for almost any other company on Earth. The machines didn't notice who was at the podium.

## The reverse DCF: the Jensen discount is already in the price

Here's my favorite part. Setup: at $153.74 and 24.15B shares, NVIDIA is a $3.71T market cap; less $23.6B of net cash, call it $3.69T of enterprise value. Base year is the trailing twelve months: $303B of revenue at a 41.9% FCF margin. Ten-year model, 10% discount rate, 3% terminal growth:

![NVDA reverse DCF: implied value per share by scenario](/images/nvda-reverse-dcf.png)

Read that table twice. The market-implied path is ~13% revenue growth for a decade — for a company that just printed +103% and guided next quarter to roughly another doubling. (This is the pre-earnings analysis, built on the $153.74 the stock took into the print; tonight's ~3% after-hours move doesn't change the shape of the math.) My explicit *moat-erosion* scenario (growth decaying to 5%, margins compressing 12 points) prices the stock at **exactly the pre-earnings price**. In other words, the market has already fired Jensen. Current pricing assumes the moat crumbles and the AI buildout limps. If you believe either CUDA holds or the buildout continues at even half speed, you're being paid to take the other side. That's not a valuation opinion — it's what the math is doing.

## Bottom line

NVIDIA without Jensen is still NVIDIA: CUDA, the chips, the margins, the demand — tonight's print just made that case for me. What it's not anymore is *Jensen's* NVIDIA — the market-maker premium is gone, and the reverse DCF says the price already reflects that. My three tells from here: who gets the permanent CEO job (a visionary vs. an operator sets the next decade), whether the checkbook actually stays closed — tonight's $390B of supply commitments says it hasn't fully — and whether Jensen loyalists start showing up in Meta/Amazon/Google org charts. Watch those, and you'll know which scenario you're in before the model does.
